Famibe no Yosshin's 1995 shoot'em up for the Sharp X68000, hundreds of bullets on the screen, at 50 frames a second on an Atari ST, STE, Mega ST or Mega STE.

The game

Cho Ren Sha 68k came out in 1995 for the Sharp X68000, a Japanese home computer built around the same Motorola 68000 as the Atari ST, but with a sprite chip, a 65,536-colour palette and a screen of 256 by 256 pixels at 55.46 frames a second. Its author, Famibe no Yosshin, filled that screen: seven stages and their bosses, a second loop harder than the first, and in the busiest moments 512 sprites at once, the game's own limit, multiplexed by its sprite driver over the chip's 128.

The ST has no sprites, sixteen colours from 512 and a screen of 200 lines. This port does not emulate the X68000. It takes the game's own program, 268,418 bytes of it, translates it to C, and runs it on the computer in the cartridge, which draws every frame and hands it to the ST. The game is the original's, step for step: every frame the ST shows is the frame the X68000 would have shown, in sixteen colours.

Twenty milliseconds

The SidecarTridge Multi-device is a Raspberry Pi RP2040 in the ST's cartridge slot: two Arm Cortex-M0+ cores at 225 MHz, 264 KB of RAM and 2 MB of flash. To the ST it is a ROM cartridge; to this port it is where the game runs. A PAL ST has fifty vertical blanks a second, 19.98 ms apart, and in each of them both machines do their part of the frame.

The cartridge runs one step of the game, the original's code translated, in 1.7 ms on average (4.7 ms at worst). It draws the picture in 5.0 ms on average, 11.9 ms in the busiest frame of a whole game, both cores at once, sixteen lines at a time; converts it to the four bit planes the ST's screen is made of in 1.2 ms; and mixes the sound. The ST copies the finished picture from the cartridge's window to its screen, flips to it at the next blank and asks for the next. A frame the cartridge finishes late is shown a blank later, never torn, and cannot change the game: over a whole game in both loops, five frames in about 149,000 were late.

For developers

The cartridge's 64 KB window at $FA0000 holds the ST's code (14,304 of its 16 KB), the command and frame-counter words, the audio buffer and, at $FA8000, the 32 KB framebuffer: 256 lines of 128 bytes, the image's 256 pixels in four planes. The RP bumps the frame counter at $FA400C as the last write of a publish; the ST copies only when its low word changes, then flips and acknowledges with a read at $FB84xx in the cartridge's third ROM area, which the RP captures with a PIO and a DMA ring. The ST cannot write to the cartridge: everything it tells the RP, it tells by reading an address.

The CPU copy stores with MOVEM predecrement, four cycles a move faster than displacement, so the RP publishes the frame in the order that copy reads it: lines 255 to 0, each in three pieces of 44, 44 and 40 bytes. The blitter reads the natural order, and the RP publishes that when the ST uses it. The drawing buffer is one byte a pixel; the conversion to planes is a hand-written Thumb routine that moves four pixels at a time with one multiply per plane, the bottom half on the second core.

Both borders open

The game was drawn for 256 lines; the ST shows 200. It shows the rest the way the demo scene does: by switching the screen to 60 Hz for a few cycles at the exact moment the video chip decides where the border starts, at the top and again at the bottom, the ST shows 274 lines. The port puts the game's 256 in the middle of them, and its 256 columns in the first 256 of the ST's 320.

That size is the ST's limit, not the port's choice. The 32 KB picture has to cross the ST's bus every frame, and the bus moves a word every eight cycles whoever moves it: 16.4 ms of the 20 are gone before a single pixel is drawn. The 68000's fastest copy takes 18.5 ms for 256 lines; the STE's blitter, which copies in pieces of sixteen lines so the keyboard is never kept waiting, 17.4 ms. On a plain ST and a Mega ST, the 68000 also plays the sound while it copies, and 256 lines no longer fit: the copy ended a quarter of a millisecond too late and the game fell to 25 frames a second. Those machines copy the top 240 lines, which hold the game and its score; the bottom sixteen stay black.

For developers

The ST's screen pages are at $60000 and $6B000, 274 lines of 160 bytes each, the image from line 9. The bottom border opens at cycle 504 of line 262: Timer-B, in event-count mode, counts display lines to the end of line 259, and its handler waits for line starts with stop and the HBL until the video counter says 262, then writes 60 Hz and 50 Hz again 69 NOPs apart. Counting to line 260, as first written, left about 50 cycles of margin, and an IKBD byte made the border miss in 1.7% of frames; a line earlier, none missed in 5,717. The top border is opened by Timer-C, which fires early in line 30 and runs on, so its count tells the handler how many lines ago it fired. Both handlers mask the keyboard and the samples while they wait, because an interrupt can start a hundred cycles late behind a MOVEM.

The CPU copy is fully unrolled: per line, three MOVEM pairs of 44, 44 and 40 bytes and a lea, 580 cycles, about 8 KB of code. A0, A4 and A7 stay out of its register lists so the sound and the interrupts can run during it. The blitter copies a word in 8 cycles against MOVEM's 8.8; the main loop sizes each piece from the two timers' counts, so no piece is running when a border must be switched.

The 68000's code, in C

The game's program is translated mechanically, not rewritten: a translator of the port's own reads the user's copy of the executable and its disassembly and writes one C function for every entry point the program has, 651 of them, 71,339 instructions, 405,013 lines of C. Compiled for the RP2040 it is 895 KB of Thumb code. Nothing was ported by hand, and there is no 68000 interpreter: the translated game steps a frame in 1.7 ms on average, against 0.54 ms for the original on a 50 MHz 68030.

It works because the game was written in C: no self-modifying code, no counted cycles, and every indirect call can be found from the program's relocations. The translation keeps the original's logic exactly, and a played game proves it. The reference is the Amiga port, which runs the original's 68000 code itself: a game recorded by the game's own recorder, through all seven stages and the second loop, matched it at 2,500 of 2,500 checkpoints and on 1,498 of 1,499 screens pixel for pixel on the Mac; on an STE, five whole games crossed 57 stage changes and 12,608 checkpoints without a single difference.

For developers

A call pushes the real return address and calls the callee's function, which returns the address its RTS popped; the caller goes on only when that is the address it pushed, else it hands it up, so code that rewrites its stack lands where the 68000 would. Flags are computed only where something may read them. A handful of patches sit where the Amiga port has its own: the sprite driver's output and its wait for the blank, the text plane's clear, the CPU-speed probe, the demo recorder's save, and the title menu reworked for the ST.

Memory is the 68000's 24-bit space in 256-byte pages through a table of page pointers, two instructions a lookup. Each 16-bit word is kept in the RP's byte order (a byte is at its address XOR 1). Pages a stage only reads stay in flash and run in place; written pages live in RAM; a write into a flash page stops the game with its address on screen. The runtime serves the X68000 calls the game makes, the IOCS keyboard, joystick, ADPCM and palette calls, the MCDRV music driver and the DOS file calls (the high scores are SCORE.DAT on the card); any other call stops the run with its name.

Where the game lives

895 KB of code and the stages' pictures do not fit beside the firmware in 2 MB of flash, let alone in 264 KB of RAM. So the game is cut into images on the microSD card: COMMON, what every stage shares (571 KB, in a 576 KB slot), and one image for each stage and for the menus, 154 to 268 KB each. Between stages, the next image is copied from the card into the flash while the ST shows the last frame: 1.5 to 1.9 seconds, and the music picks up again in step. The game's variables live in RAM, in 350 pages of 256 bytes found in three places: a 48 KB array, the part of the drawing buffer a frame drawn in bands leaves free, and the cartridge window's spare room.

tools/xlat/rp_store.py makes the images from 21 runs of the whole game on the Mac: a played game through both loops, a game to its game over and name entry, every menu with every item changed, the attract demo, and a game from each stage and each boss. Each run records which functions every stage enters and which pages it writes and reads. A function belongs to a stage when only that stage runs it, to COMMON when more than one does; a page that a stage writes, or reads after another wrote it, goes to RAM; the rest stays in the images. Each image is compiled for the RP and linked at its flash address against the firmware's own ELF, so it calls the firmware's runtime directly: an image fits one firmware build only, and carries that build's ID in its header. The whole run, from the files to the card, takes about three minutes on a Mac.

A stage changes when the game calls a function the slot does not hold: COMMON's table says which image has it. The copy erases in 64 KB blocks, about 150 ms each with the interrupts off, programs through a 4 KB buffer, writes the image's first page last and checks its CRC-32, so a copy cut short is never taken for a whole one. Each page keeps one slot of the RAM pool in every stage, so a stage change moves no page; 325 of the 345 usable slots are used.

Sixteen colours

The game's palettes hold 117 colours, and a frame of play uses about 57 of them; the ST shows sixteen, from 512, on every machine. For each stage, the colours are sorted offline into sixteen groups by how they move over the stage, so the entries that cycle or fade together share an ST colour. Then, every frame, the cartridge works out the sixteen colours from the game's palette as it is at that moment, in about 50 µs: the colour cycling and the fades play as palette changes, as on the X68000.

For developers

Each ST colour is the mean of its entries' current colours in linear light, weighted by how much of the screen each covers, rounded channel by channel to the nearest of the ST's eight levels. tools/xlat/palette_fit.py makes the grouping from a run's per-pixel dumps and writes the same integer arithmetic the RP runs, so the Mac predicts the cartridge's frames exactly: on the ST, held frames differ from the Mac's in no pixel and no colour.

Sound

The effects are the X68000's own: 31 ADPCM samples, decoded as its MSM6258 chip decodes them. The music is the game's twelve songs as the Amiga port renders them from the original's music driver, cut into an intro and a loop that joins itself without a gap, and resampled for each kind of ST. An STE or a Mega STE plays it through its DMA sound chip, at 25,033 samples a second; a plain ST or a Mega ST through its YM2149, whose three channels, tied together inside the ST, make an eight-bit voice at about 7,700 samples a second, written by the 68000 between its moves while it copies the frame.

For developers

The resampler is a Kaiser-windowed sinc, 128 phases, 24 taps to 25,033 Hz and 48 to 7,777 Hz, cut at 90% of the lower Nyquist; the loop is the third of three played after the intro, so it joins both the intro's end and its own. The effects are mixed in one at a time, as the game chooses them by priority, read from the card a sector at a time; a whole game plays 5,934 of them and starts 21 songs, at the Mac's frames exactly.

The DMA chip loops over a 4 KB ring at $76000 in ST RAM. Every blank the ST reads where the chip plays, reports it to the RP with an address read, and copies the cartridge's mirror up to three blanks (1,536 bytes) ahead of it; the RP keeps the mirror 1,856 bytes ahead of the chip. On the YM path, the copy writes a sample after every fifth MOVEM pair: six bytes through movep, the three channels' volumes from a table fitted to the levels measured on a real ST, 153 samples during the copy and a few more around the borders. The ST reports where it is, and the RP writes ahead of it and resamples to the rate the ST actually achieves, so no sample is dropped or held. A curve, 127 tanh(2x)/tanh(2), raises the YM's quiet levels about 6 dB to match the DMA chip's loudness.

The card's files

None of the game is in the app, in its repository or on this site's server. The converter at the top of this page is the cartridge's own install code, compiled to WebAssembly: it reads the Amiga port's public repository zip in your browser, checks every file it needs by size and CRC-32, decodes the effects, resamples the songs for both kinds of sound chip, and unlocks the game's images. Those are published only encrypted, under a key that only the repository gives. Then it writes about 51 MB to the card.

The game is GAME.TOS on the cartridge's drive c:. Each time it starts, the cartridge checks the card: the images made for its firmware, the manifest, the sound for the machine's chip. If anything is missing, the ST says what, in red, with a QR code to this page for its own release, and checks again every second, so the game starts as soon as the card is right.

For developers

The zip is read in place from its central directory and inflated with miniz's inflater; a member that comes back damaged is read again, up to three times. The images' key is the SHA-256 of binaries/x68000/sz2.hex as the zip holds it; each image is ChaCha20 under a nonce from its name and the firmware's build ID, with a 64-byte header that names both and carries the plain bytes' CRC-32, so a wrong key is caught. The keyed pack is an asset of each release, never in the repository; the site's build checks it was made for the release's firmware and that the WebAssembly makes every file the cartridge's code makes, byte for byte.

GAME.TOS enters supervisor mode, checks that its memory holds the fixed RAM the ST's code uses ($60000 to $78000: on a 512 KB ST, TOS's screen starts right after it), runs on its own stack, switches a medium-resolution desktop to low and gives the desktop back its resolution and palette when the game ends. A card taken out to be written on a computer is initialised and mounted again when it comes back: the cartridge has no card-detect line, so it learns of it from a failed read.

Building it

The app is a UF2 for the Multi-device, built from the repository with the ARM GNU Toolchain and the Atari ST toolkit; its README gives the commands and a release's steps.

To play, there is no UF2 to install by hand: Booster, the Multi-device's own app manager, downloads the app from the SidecarTridge store and installs it, and its updates too.

The firmware carries no game code. A build's ID, its commit and whether its tree was clean, names the images it fits; the converter page and the cartridge's files page both show it. Host tests run the firmware's own units under the address and undefined-behaviour sanitizers on every pull request: the renderer against random scenes of up to 600 sprites, the zip reader, the install, the sound converter, the keyed images, both copies' layouts, and the ST's and the RP's shared constants.
